Abstract—Identifying molecular structures from Raman spec-
tra is a high-impact applied data mining problem for real-
time chemical analysis, yet it remains a difficult inverse task
because spectra are noisy, domain-shifted, and only weakly
tied to unique structures. We present SpectraExpert, an ap-
plied retrieval-analysis-reasoning system that operationalizes the
workflow of a human spectroscopist. Given a query Raman
spectrum, the framework first retrieves candidate molecules via
a contrastive SpectrumTransformer encoder trained on 129,817
QM9S spectra, then matches detected peaks to 181 empirical
Nakamoto group-frequency rules, and finally deploys an LLM to
evaluate candidates and propose structural modifications guided
by spectroscopic evidence. We evaluate five frontier LLMs across
four experimental settings on a reproducible zero-shot cross-
domain benchmark using a QM9S training database with ≤9
heavy atoms and a ChEMBL test set with up to 21 heavy atoms.
Retrieval baselines achieve mean graph edit distance of 16.93
on 211 test samples. On randomly sampled LLM evaluation
batches, the agentic pipeline includes individual predictions
with GED as low as 8.0, while Claude Sonnet 4.6 maintains
100% valid-SMILES output in direct and agentic modes and
96% in knowledge mode. The study provides both an end-to-
end application prototype and a benchmark for spectroscopic
molecular identification. Our analysis shows that structured-
output reliability is the dominant deployment bottleneck, and that
the framework delivers interpretable, expert-aligned reasoning
inaccessible to retrieval alone.

I. INTRODUCTION
Raman spectroscopy [1] is a non-destructive analytical
technique that probes molecular vibrational modes through
inelastic light scattering. Since vibrational frequencies are
determined by molecular structures and chemical bonds, Ra-
man spectra provide rich information about the composition
and structure of chemical compounds. Each molecule exhibits
characteristic spectral signatures, commonly referred to as
molecular fingerprints, which enable qualitative identifica-
tion and quantitative analysis [2], [3]. Compared with other
analytical techniques, including Mass Spectrometry (MS),
Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR), Infrared Spectroscopy
(IR), and Ultraviolet–Visible Spectroscopy (UV–Vis), Raman
spectroscopy offers several advantages such as minimal sample
preparation, compatibility with aqueous environments, high

Recent advances in portable Raman instrumentation and
Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS) [5] have sig-
nificantly reduced the cost and complexity of spectral acqui-
sition, enabling broad deployment in chemistry, biology, en-
vironmental monitoring, pharmaceutical analysis, and medical
diagnostics [6]. Related optical and vibrational spectroscopieshave also been explored for health monitoring and diagnostic
applications [7], [8]. As a result, automatic interpretation of
Raman spectra has become increasingly important for real-
time and on-site molecular identification. This setting fits
the applied data mining agenda: it requires integrating large
spectral databases, machine-learned representations, domain
knowledge, and reliable decision support in a practical sci-
entific workflow.
Despite its practical importance, identifying molecular
structures directly from Raman spectra remains a challenging
problem. Raman spectra are influenced by experimental con-
ditions, molecular conformations, and environmental factors,
resulting in significant spectral variability. Furthermore, large-
scale paired datasets of experimental spectra and molecular
structures remain scarce. Consequently, most existing machine
learning approaches focus on spectrum prediction, classifica-
tion, or functional-group identification within narrow domains
[9], [10], while accurate molecular-level identification remains
largely unsolved.
Recent progress in Large Language Models (LLMs) has
demonstrated remarkable reasoning capabilities across scien-
tific domains. This raises an intriguing question:
Can LLMs perform expert-level spectroscopic reasoning
and accurately identify molecular structures from Raman
spectra?
Our preliminary investigation reveals that directly applying
LLMs to Raman identification often leads to unsatisfactory
performance for two fundamental reasons.
First, molecular identification is an extremely large search-
space problem. Given only spectral observations, an LLM
must reason over an enormous number of possible molecular
structures. Without suitable constraints, the model frequently
produces chemically implausible candidates or hallucinates
structures that are inconsistent with the observed spectra.
Second, successful spectroscopic analysis requires domain-
specific knowledge that is not reliably encoded in general-
purpose LLMs. Human spectroscopists rely heavily on em-
pirical correlations between vibrational modes, characteristic
peak regions, and molecular functional groups. LLMs often
fail to consistently apply such spectroscopic principles, leading
to incorrect structural interpretations.
To address these challenges, we propose SpectraExpert, a
retrieval-enhanced reasoning system for molecular identifica-
tion from Raman spectra. Inspired by the workflow of human
spectroscopists, our method decomposes the identification
process into three stages. First, a spectral retrieval module
narrows the search space by identifying structurally related
candidate molecules based on spectral similarity. Second,
a Nakamoto-rule spectral analysis stage matches observed
peaks to empirical group-frequency rules and highlights dis-
criminative functional-group evidence. Finally, a knowledge-
guided reasoning module combines spectroscopic rules, do-
main knowledge, and structured rationale generation to infer
the most plausible molecular structure.
This design significantly reduces hallucinations while im-
proving the utilization of spectroscopy knowledge during
reasoning. Rather than generating molecular structures from
scratch, SpectraExpert performs structured hypothesis refine-
ment over a small set of chemically plausible candidates,
closely mimicking the decision-making process of human
experts.
The main applied contributions of this work are summarized
as follows:
• We formulate Raman-based molecular identification as
an applied data mining problem that combines spectral
representation learning, candidate retrieval, and expert-
guided reasoning for chemical decision support.
• We propose SpectraExpert, an end-to-end system that
integrates spectral retrieval, candidate disambiguation,
Nakamoto rule matching, and LLM-guided structural
refinement.
• We construct a reproducible cross-domain benchmark
using QM9S [9] for retrieval training and ChEMBL
Raman spectra [11] for evaluation, enabling systematic
analysis of generalization under realistic domain shift.
• Extensive experiments and ablations quantify retrieval
quality, structured-output reliability, model compliance,
and practical failure modes across five frontier LLMs.
Our results suggest that combining retrieval and scientific
reasoning provides a promising direction for deploying LLMs
in spectroscopy and other scientific inverse problems, while
also clarifying the engineering constraints that must be ad-
dressed before real-world deployment. All code and data are
available at https://github.com/AndrewLuan/SpectraExpert.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Retrieval-Based Molecular Identification
Molecular identification from spectroscopic measurements
is a fundamental problem in chemistry, materials science, and
life sciences. Traditional machine learning methods often map
spectra directly to molecular properties, functional groups,
or structures. Deep architectures such as RamanNet [12],
together with larger spectral resources such as the Raman and
infrared extension of ChEMBL [11] and RamanBench [13],
have advanced data-driven Raman analysis.
However, molecular identification remains a difficult inverse
problem: similar structures can produce highly similar spectra,
and experimental conditions introduce additional variability.
RamanBench further shows that existing models often suffer
from limited cross-dataset generalization [13]. These issues
make direct spectrum-to-structure prediction unreliable when
the candidate space is large.
Retrieval-based methods reduce this search space before
downstream inference. Related retrieval-augmented scientific
systems, such as MatExpert [14], show that combining re-
trieval with expert-like reasoning can improve efficiency and
reliability in large scientific search spaces. Following this
idea, SpectraExpert first retrieves structurally plausible spectral
candidates and then performs knowledge-guided reasoning to
identify the target molecule.B. Large Language Models for Scientific Reasoning
Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown strong capa-
bilities in reasoning, planning, and knowledge-intensive tasks.
Chain-of-thought prompting [15] and knowledge-augmented
scientific reasoners [16] indicate that intermediate explana-
tions and external domain knowledge can improve complex
scientific problem solving.
Tool-augmented LLMs provide an important precedent.
MRKL systems decompose tasks across neural and symbolic
modules [17], Toolformer shows that language models can
learn when to call external tools [18], and ReAct interleaves
reasoning traces with actions [19]. In chemistry, domain-
specific and tool-augmented LLM systems have been explored
for diverse tasks. ChemLLM [20] trains on structured chemi-
cal knowledge, ChemCrow [21] integrates external chemistry
tools for synthesis planning, safety checks, and drug-discovery
tasks, and autonomous chemistry agents coordinate scientific
workflows through iterative reasoning and tool use [22].
SpectraExpert is related to these systems in its use of
tool-like components, but its target and evidence structure
are different. ChemCrow delegates to general chemistry tools
such as reaction predictors, molecular search utilities, and
property calculators; the central challenge is selecting and
sequencing tools. SpectraExpert instead solves a spectrum-
conditioned inverse problem: the external modules are a
learned Raman retriever and a curated Nakamoto rule matcher,
and the LLM must reconcile candidate structures with peak-
level spectroscopic evidence. This makes the contribution
less a general chemistry agent and more a domain-specific
data-mining workflow for weakly supervised, cross-domain
molecular identification.
These systems also reveal a key limitation: LLM reliability
deteriorates when the hypothesis space is large or domain con-
straints are weakly represented. Spectroscopic molecular iden-
tification remains relatively unexplored in this context, because
it requires joint reasoning over spectral evidence, candidate
structures, and spectroscopy-specific rules. Our work addresses
this gap by integrating spectral retrieval and knowledge-guided
reasoning for Raman-based molecular identification.
III. PRELIMINARIES
A. Raman Spectroscopy and Molecular Similarity
Raman spectroscopy probes molecular vibrational modes
through the inelastic scattering of light. From a physical
perspective, the vibrational frequencies of a molecule are
determined by its atomic composition, chemical bonds, and
geometric structure. Consequently, the Raman spectrum can be
regarded as a projection of the underlying molecular structure
into the spectral domain.
Although the mapping from molecular structure to Raman
spectrum is highly complex, a fundamental observation from
vibrational spectroscopy is that structurally similar molecules
often exhibit similar spectral patterns. Molecules sharing com-
mon functional groups or bonding configurations tend to pos-
sess related vibrational modes, which produce characteristic
peaks in similar spectral regions. For example, compounds
containing aromatic rings, carbonyl groups, or hydroxyl groups
usually exhibit identifiable spectral signatures associated with
these structural motifs.
This property establishes an important relationship between
molecular similarity and spectral similarity. While distinct
molecules may occasionally generate partially overlapping
spectra, compounds with similar structures generally occupy
neighboring regions in spectral space. As a result, Raman
spectra provide a meaningful basis for retrieving candidate
molecules that are structurally related to an unknown target.
However, spectral similarity alone is insufficient for unique
molecular identification. Many organic molecules share com-
mon functional groups and therefore exhibit highly similar
spectral fingerprints. Human spectroscopists typically resolve
such ambiguities by comparing subtle peak differences, ex-
amining characteristic vibrational regions, and incorporating
domain knowledge about molecular structures. This process
can be viewed as a sequential hypothesis-refinement proce-
dure: candidate structures are first proposed based on spectral
similarity and then progressively eliminated through expert
reasoning.
Our method is motivated by this physical and analytical
workflow. The retrieval stage exploits the correlation between
structural similarity and spectral similarity to narrow the
search space, while the reasoning stage analyzes discriminative
spectral features and spectroscopic knowledge to distinguish
among competing candidates. This design enables the model to
mimic the decision-making process of human spectroscopists
while maintaining consistency with the underlying physics of
Raman spectroscopy.
B. Cheminformatics Evaluation Metrics
We report molecular similarity using graph edit distance
(GED), Tanimoto similarity, exact match, and SMILES valid-
ity. GED measures the minimum number of atom or bond edit
operations required to transform a predicted molecular graph
into the ground-truth graph; lower values indicate closer graph
structure. Tanimoto similarity compares molecular fingerprints
and emphasizes shared local substructures, following standard
chemoinformatics practice based on molecular fingerprints and
toolkit canonicalization [23]; higher values indicate greater
fingerprint overlap. Exact match is computed only after canon-
icalization and therefore represents the strictest criterion.
SMILES validity is treated as a reliability gate rather
than a primary chemistry achievement. In cheminformatics,
generating syntactically valid SMILES is a basic require-
ment for downstream property prediction, database lookup,
or molecular editing. We therefore use the Parsed metric to
diagnose whether an LLM can obey the required structured-
output interface, while interpreting GED and Tanimoto as the
substantive identification metrics.
C. Applied Data-Mining Setting
The data-mining contribution of this work lies in turn-
ing Raman identification into a reproducible retrieval-analysis-reasoning benchmark under domain shift. The train-
ing database contains simulated QM9S spectra for small
molecules, whereas the evaluation queries are ChEMBL Ra-
man spectra with larger and more application-oriented struc-
tures. This separation intentionally stresses cross-domain gen-
eralization and prevents the system from succeeding through
memorized database overlap.
From a mining perspective, the task combines three op-
erations that are difficult to evaluate in isolation: represen-
tation learning over dense spectra, retrieval from a large
candidate corpus, and evidence-grounded decision support.
From a chemistry perspective, the same setup exposes the
gap between syntactic molecular generation and chemically
meaningful identification. We therefore position SpectraExpert
as a benchmarked application workflow rather than as a claim
that LLMs alone solve Raman-based molecular elucidation.
IV. METHOD
A. Problem Formulation
Given a Raman spectrum of an unknown compound,
S = {(wi,Ii)}Ns
i=1,
where wi and Ii denote the Raman shift and corresponding
intensity at the i-th spectral point, our goal is to identify the
molecular structure that generated the observed spectrum and
predict its corresponding molecular representation, such as a
SMILES string.
Formally, let M denote the space of all candidate molecules.
Molecular identification from Raman spectra can be viewed as
learning an inverse mapping
f : S → M, M̂ = f(S),
where S denotes the space of Raman spectra and M̂ ∈ M is
the predicted target molecule represented by a SMILES string.
Compared with the forward problem of spectrum prediction,
this inverse problem is substantially more challenging. A
single molecule deterministically produces a spectrum under
given conditions, whereas the reverse mapping is inherently
ambiguous. Different molecules may share common functional
groups and vibrational modes, leading to highly similar spec-
tral fingerprints. Furthermore, the molecular search space is
extremely large, making exhaustive reasoning over all possible
structures computationally infeasible.
Human spectroscopists rarely identify compounds by di-
rectly inferring a structure from a spectrum. Instead, they typi-
cally follow a hierarchical workflow. They first retrieve a small
set of compounds exhibiting similar spectral characteristics,
then compare discriminative spectral features among candidate
structures, and finally apply spectroscopic knowledge and
empirical rules to determine the most plausible molecular
hypothesis.
Inspired by this expert workflow, we formulate Raman-
based molecular identification as a retrieval-enhanced scien-
tific reasoning problem. Rather than directly generating molec-
ular structures from spectra, our objective is to progressively
reduce uncertainty through retrieval, candidate refinement, and
knowledge-guided reasoning.
B. Overview of SpectraExpert
SpectraExpert decomposes molecular identification from
Raman spectra into a three-stage pipeline that mirrors the
workflow of a human spectroscopist: (1) a retrieval stage that
finds candidate molecules from a spectral database via con-
trastive representation learning, (2) a Nakamoto-rule spectral
analysis stage that matches detected peaks against a structured
knowledge base of empirical group-frequency rules, and (3) a
reasoning stage where an LLM evaluates candidates, selects
the best match, or proposes structural modifications. Figure 1
provides an overview of the architecture.

D. Stage 2: Spectral Analysis with Nakamoto Rules
1) Knowledge Base: We compiled a structured knowledge
base of 181 empirical group-frequency rules by digitizing
and organizing group-frequency tables from Nakamoto’s au-
thoritative textbook Infrared and Raman Spectra of Inorganic
and Coordination Compounds [25] and cross-checking organic
functional-group ranges against Socrates’ tabulation of char-
acteristic IR and Raman frequencies [26].
Each rule contains:
• A unique identifier (e.g., GFQ-042);
• Functional-group description;
• Characteristic frequency range;
• Confidence level;
• Rule category;
• Additional notes and spectral details.
Rules were normalized into a common schema with fields
for rule ID, lower and upper frequency bounds, vibrational
mode, functional group, source note, and confidence tag. Con-
fidence tags were assigned during curation from the specificity
of the frequency range and whether the source explicitly
marks the band as Raman-active. For example, a rule such
as GFQ-042 links a characteristic frequency interval to a
candidate functional group and is later cited verbatim in the
LLM knowledge summary.
2) Peak-to-Rule Matching: For each detected peak at po-
sition p, we search the knowledge base for rules whose

E. Stage 3: LLM-Guided Reasoning
1) System Prompt and Persona: The LLM is instructed to
act as an expert spectroscopist and to ground every functional-
group claim in the supplied Nakamoto rule IDs. Unlike general
chemistry agents, this stage does not call reaction planners or
property tools; it only reconciles the fixed evidence packet
from retrieval and rule matching.
2) Structured Rationale Generation:
Given detected peaks, top-K candidates, and matched rules,
the LLM performs functional-group identification, candidate
evaluation, consensus assessment, and a final decision among
SELECT, MODIFY, and UNCERTAIN. The output schema
contains the decision, selected index or modified SMILES,
confidence, and explanation. Deterministic decoding is used
when available, and invalid or unparsable SMILES are counted
through the Parsed metric. The decision policy and an example
response are detailed in Appendix A.
3) Modification Fallback: When confidence falls below 0.6,
or when the model returns UNCERTAIN or MODIFY, a
second prompt asks for local chemical edits to the closest
candidate while preserving as much scaffold as possible. The
resulting SMILES is canonicalized and validated. We treat this
fallback as a stress test of chemistry-aware editing rather than
a guaranteed improvement mechanism, and therefore report

V. EXPERIMENTS
A. Datasets and Benchmarks
Evaluating Raman-based molecular identification requires
both large-scale spectral data for representation learning and
an independent benchmark for assessing generalization. To
support reproducible applied evaluation, we employ two com-
plementary datasets with separated roles: QM9S is used only
to build the retrieval database and train the spectral encoder,
whereas ChEMBL Raman spectra are reserved for out-of-
domain testing.
a) QM9S Training Dataset.: The retrieval model is
trained using the QM9S dataset, a large-scale quantum chem-
istry database containing Raman spectra and molecular struc-
tures for over one hundred thousand organic molecules. Each
sample consists of a molecular representation (SMILES) and
a corresponding Raman spectrum represented on a uniform
wavenumber grid spanning from 500 to 4000 cm−1
with a
resolution of 1 cm−1
. The resulting spectrum is therefore
represented as a 3501-dimensional intensity vector. Figure 2
illustrates the distributions of molecular complexity and spec-
tral intensity within the QM9S dataset.
The primary motivation for using QM9S is its scale and
structural diversity. Since contrastive retrieval requires learning
a semantically meaningful spectral embedding space, a large
number of molecular examples is essential for capturing the
relationship between spectral fingerprints and molecular struc-
tures. The resulting database serves as the retrieval corpus used
in Stage 1 of SpectraExpert. The global spectral distribution
of the dataset is visualized in Figure 3, where several high-
density Raman-active regions can be observed.
b) ChEMBL Raman Evaluation Dataset.: To evaluate
the complete workflow, we adopt the Raman subset from A
Dataset of Raman and Infrared Spectra as an Extension to
the ChEMBL [11].
The ChEMBL Raman dataset is used exclusively for evalu-
ation and shares no molecular overlap with the QM9S training
set. Since the two datasets originate from distinct molecular
collections, they exhibit substantially different structural distri-
butions and spectral characteristics. Consequently, evaluation
on the ChEMBL dataset provides a rigorous assessment of
whether a Raman identification system can transfer from a
curated quantum-chemistry corpus to larger, more application-
oriented molecules.
This benchmark is intentionally harder than closed-library
spectral search. A domain-specific tool with the exact target
spectrum in its library would be expected to win by direct
matching, but such a setting does not test molecular inference.
Our evaluation instead asks whether a system trained and
indexed on small QM9S molecules can provide useful can-
didate hypotheses, explanations, and edits for out-of-domain
ChEMBL targets. This framing is central to the applied data-
mining claim: the task measures transfer, retrieval quality, and
evidence use under a controlled corpus shift.
B. Experimental Setup
We evaluate four categories of methods on the SpectraEx-
pert SMILES-from-spectra prediction task. Retrieval methods
(KNN, Encoder) retrieve the top-10 database candidates by
spectral similarity across all 211 queries in the constructed
ChEMBL test set. LLM Direct generates SMILES from spec-
tral peak data alone on a randomly sampled 50-query batch
from this test set. LLM Knowledge augments peak data with
matched Nakamoto group-frequency rules and is evaluated
on a 50-query batch randomly sampled from this test set.
Agentic combines retrieval, Nakamoto rule matching, multi-
step LLM reasoning, and a structural modification fallback,
evaluated on a randomly sampled 20-query batch from the
same test set. The LLM batches were sampled from the
constructed evaluation set rather than selected by molecule
length or difficulty; therefore, aggregate GED values acrossLLM settings should be interpreted as batch-level results
unless the same query batch is used. Model-source citations
are shown directly next to model names in the result tables.
Performance is measured by graph edit distance (GED;
lower is better), Tanimoto similarity (higher is better), and
the fraction of outputs yielding valid, parseable SMILES
(Parsed). Generated SMILES are parsed and canonicalized
before evaluation; invalid SMILES are excluded from GED
and Tanimoto summaries and are instead counted through
the Parsed metric. The reported N denotes the number of
attempted queries, not the number of valid outputs. GED is
computed on molecular graphs after canonicalization using
the same unit-cost edit model for all methods; atom labels
and bond labels are retained, and implicit hydrogens are
used consistently across predictions and ground truth. For
retrieval methods, GED is the minimum across the top-10
candidates (best@10); for LLM and agentic methods it is
computed from the single predicted SMILES. Exact match
denotes equality after molecular-graph canonicalization.

Full results are reported in Table I. No method achieves

The strongest numbers should be interpreted cautiously. The

estimate of benchmark difficulty. The practical conclusion is
not that one LLM universally solves the task, but that rule-
grounded prompts can sometimes produce strong hypotheses
while structured-output failures and batch variation remain
major limitations.
D. Retrieval Baselines: Encoder vs. KNN
The encoder and KNN retrieval methods perform virtually
identically. GED means differ by only 0.11 (16.93 vs. 17.04),
within noise. The encoder is faster (14.9s end-to-end), making
it the preferable baseline for downstream comparisons.
E. LLM Direct and LLM Knowledge
LLM Direct and LLM Knowledge are evaluated on ran-
domly sampled 50-query batches from the same constructed
ChEMBL test set. Raw GED values should be interpreted
as setting-specific batch summaries; strict paired comparisons

Table II provides a batch-controlled comparison by evaluat-

used for LLM Direct. LLM Direct achieves GED values

24.48) but falling slightly short of the Encoder best@10 oracle

database, yet cannot yet surpass a well-tuned retrieval re-
ranker on this sampled cross-domain batch.
On the randomly sampled LLM Knowledge batch, o3
achieves GED 13.52 (Table I), showing that Nakamoto rule
augmentation can produce strong valid-only predictions on
sampled ChEMBL queries. Parse rate remains a key dif-
ferentiator: Claude-Sonnet achieves 96% compliance with
knowledge-augmented prompts vs. 20% for Gemini-2.5-Flash.
The counterintuitive Tanimoto result for Gemini (0.000) in
LLM Knowledge mode indicates systematic hallucination: its
parsed SMILES bear no fingerprint overlap with ground truth
despite non-trivial GED values.
F. SMILES Output Validity
Parse rates vary substantially across models (Table III).
Claude-Sonnet-4-6 achieves perfect compliance in direct and
agentic modes, whereas Gemini-2.5-Flash fails frequently in
unstructured settings. The agentic schema improves Gemini
validity (0.20 → 1.00) but degrades GPT-5 (0.38 → 0.05),
confirming that parseability is a necessary interface require-
ment rather than a standalone measure of chemical accuracy.
G. Agentic Pipeline
On the sampled 20-query agentic batch, the full pipeline
includes individual predictions with GED as low as 8.0 for
Claude, DeepSeek, and o3, showing that reasoning can suc-
ceed when retrieval supplies a structurally proximate candi-
date. Table IV shows that SELECTED decisions consistently
outperform MODIFIED decisions; the fallback is mainly trig-
gered when the QM9S–ChEMBL domain gap leaves all re-
trieved candidates structurally distant. Gemini always invokes
MODIFY, while GPT-5 is mostly UNCERTAIN in multi-turn
use.
A notable negative result is that modification degrades
average GED relative to the SELECT path (+4.86 for Claude,
+2.98 for DeepSeek, +1.23 for o3), because unconstrained
SMILES editing from a poor starting point is itself hard. This
highlights the need for calibrated handoffs and chemistry-
aware constrained editing in future retrieval-augmented sci-
entific workflows.
H. Summary and Practical Recommendations
Across all settings, retrieval is the most stable baseline:
the encoder achieves mean best@10 GED 16.93 over all
211 queries, and retrieval best@10 remains lower than every
direct LLM result on the paired direct batch. LLMs add com-
plementary capabilities rather than replacing retrieval: direct
prompting gives zero-shot structures, Nakamoto-rule prompt-
ing improves valid-only predictions on sampled batches, and
the agentic pipeline provides interpretable rule-grounded traces
with individual GED values as low as 8.0. For deployment,

VI. CONCLUSION
We presented SpectraExpert, an applied data mining system
for Raman-based molecular identification. The framework
combines a contrastive SpectrumTransformer retriever, 181
Nakamoto-derived group-frequency rules, and LLM-guided
structured reasoning with a conservative modification fallback.
Experiments show that the QM9S–ChEMBL domain gap
remains severe, but explicit spectroscopic evidence improves
interpretability and can improve valid-only GED on sampled
batches. Future work should extend the retrieval database to
larger molecules and replace unconstrained SMILES editing
with chemistry-aware constrained generation.
